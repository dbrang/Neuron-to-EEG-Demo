Biology & model

Population rhythms: what changes?

The Population rhythms tab simulates 64 excitatory pyramidal cells and 16 inhibitory interneurons with sparse recurrent connections. Each cell receives independent Poisson background inputs: defaults are 200 excitatory axons at 2 Hz each and 50 inhibitory axons at 5 Hz each. These are per-axon mean rates, giving 400 and 250 incoming events/second per cell. They are not two axons firing hundreds of times per second. The simulated interneurons can fire faster than individual background axons; their rates are outcomes of the circuit.

Membrane threshold crossings produce spikes, which reach other cells after 3 ms. Pyramidal spiking adds a 50 pA adaptation current that decays over the selected recovery time (default 70 ms). Inhibition and recovery limit recruitment before the next population event. No periodic input-rate envelope, target-frequency clock, or sinusoid is injected. The default random trial produces an alpha-range peak, but changing drive, coupling, recovery or seed can change or abolish that rhythm. This is one simplified recurrent mechanism, not a validated or unique explanation of human alpha. Cortical and thalamic contributions depend on region and state.

The intracellular trace follows the first pyramidal cell; the two lower panels show actual spikes from all 64 excitatory and 16 inhibitory cells, one row per cell. Vertical clusters show shared timing; missing marks show skipped cycles. Cells differ in threshold, background strength and random input timing. The E and I cartoons and molecular insets remain in the Single-neuron circuit tab.

For responsive rendering, the field calculation samples 24 of the 64 pyramidal cells and gives each a weight of 2,048/24. Six drawn pyramids illustrate the patch. This representative scaling preserves the simulated temporal structure but does not simulate 2,048 independent neurons or their anatomical connectivity. Interneuron transmembrane fields, adaptation currents as separate spatial sources, spike fields and presynaptic Ca²⁺ fields are omitted. The local field and scalp signal both derive from the same modeled pyramidal synaptic currents and equal opposite returns.

From cortical tissue to the scalp electrode

The overview locates the enlarged cortical patch inside a schematic head. Pyramidal dendrites point toward the cortical surface and axons extend toward white matter; cell drawings and folds are illustrative. Electrode 3 touches the outside of the scalp. The head calculation uses four concentric spherical conductors: brain, CSF, skull and scalp. Outer radii are 78, 80, 86 and 92 mm, and conductivities are 0.3, 1.79, 0.01 and 0.43 S/m, respectively. Source somata lie near a 76 mm radius, with the same 0.2 mm per model-unit spacing used locally. These are chosen teaching parameters, not a reconstruction of a participant.

The scalp forward calculation expands each source/return pair into 100 spherical harmonics, enforcing continuous potential and normal current at each tissue boundary and zero normal current at the scalp surface. Scalp voltage is relative to the mean scalp potential; the local point probe uses the original homogeneous conductor and ideal distant reference. Both channels can be shown raw or with the same 0.5–40 Hz filter. Tissue conduction is instantaneous in this quasistatic approximation: skull and distance reduce and spatially spread the signal, without adding a slow traveling wave or arbitrary time delay. For this small patch the scalp amplitude is small; separately labeled scales make it visible without artificially boosting the calculated voltage.

The E/I source layout lumps return currents at somata; no realistic folded-cortex solution, active dendritic channels, NMDA/GABA-B receptors, synaptic plasticity, or thalamus is simulated. A clearer alpha-like trace does not establish a biologically unique generator.

Single-neuron circuit: reading the insets

Na⁺ and K⁺ both carry positive charge. Near rest, Na⁺ enters because its concentration and electrical gradients both favor entry. K⁺ leaves because its outward concentration gradient outweighs its inward electrical pull. More positive charge enters than leaves through the excitatory channels, so their combined current depolarizes the dendrite. The two ion rows depict the same class of glutamate-gated pore. A negative outward current means positive charge moving inward, not negative ions entering.

At the default GABA reversal potential, Cl⁻ enters with negative charge. Conventional current points the opposite way. Positive extracellular voltage near a contact or the soma does not mean the cell interior is depolarizing.

What is a return current?

Imagine a complete electrical circuit: current enters one patch of membrane, spreads inside the cell, crosses other membrane regions, and returns through the surrounding fluid. This balancing flow is the return current. It includes membrane leak and capacitive charging. The drawing places the returns near the soma; real return currents are distributed.

What is being modeled?

The neuron-count control selects 1–4,096 pyramidal targets. At most 12 representative pyramids are drawn. In circuit mode, targets are identical and receive the same two input trains. In rhythm mode the two drawn input neurons represent excitatory and inhibitory input populations; their rate envelopes are coordinated, while individual spike timings differ. Target cells have no recurrent or ephaptic coupling. Their extracellular sources are summed; adding cells does not increase the membrane voltage of an individual target. In the synchronous circuit mode, the net dipole scales with count; electrode voltage also depends on location and need not scale identically.

A three-compartment conductance-based integrate-and-fire neuron: two passive dendritic regions are electrically coupled to a soma. Excitatory glutamatergic and inhibitory GABAergic contacts terminate on separate dendrites. Their reversal potentials and instantaneous local voltages determine current direction and magnitude. The branches are an illustrative pyramidal morphology; they are not a reconstructed cable tree.

The initial soma and dendrites rest at −65 mV. The axon-initial-segment threshold is represented by a somatic threshold of −50 mV. Crossing it triggers a displayed spike, reset, and 3 ms refractory interval. Leak restores voltage toward rest, while capacitance and dendritic coupling govern its time course. The spike shape is illustrative, not a simulation of individual voltage-gated sodium and potassium channels.

Each presynaptic spike reaches its terminal after 6 ms; transmitter release follows 1 ms later. The transmitter then takes an illustrative 0.6 ms to cross the cleft and bind. New synaptic conductance rises only after binding. When inputs overlap, channels opened by earlier releases can remain active while new transmitter is in transit. Calcium entry and vesicle fusion are shown schematically. The calcium-entry interval is 3 ms of biological time and takes 6 seconds of motion on screen in Auto playback (2,000× slower), plus any guided explanation pauses. Transmitter release begins 1 ms after calcium entry starts, so the animations overlap. Synaptic conductances rise and decay over milliseconds, representing channel activation and transmitter clearance. Ion dots indicate direction; their number and speed are illustrative, not measured flux. An illustrative current decomposition splits the excitatory conductance into illustrative Na⁺ and K⁺ components using reversal potentials of +60 and −90 mV and conductance fractions 0.6 and 0.4. This preserves the combined 0 mV reversal and calculated net synaptic current; it does not impose a fixed ion-exchange ratio.

Biological time versus playback

0.2 Hz really means one spike every 5 seconds of biological time. EPSPs are not stretched to last seconds in the model. Auto playback also holds biological time for 3 seconds at each new stage for the E and I inputs, and at the first output spike. Repeated stages continue without repeated holds. The checkbox disables these holds; Continue now skips a hold; Replay Ca²⁺ resets the stage holds. Auto playback slows the display near events so the causal chain can be seen; the displayed biological clock remains accurate. At equal rates, phase is the inhibitory train’s delay within the common cycle. With different rates, the two trains drift relative to each other.

Connection strengths are chosen to make summation visible. Each drawn contact represents an effective connection, potentially pooling several real contacts. The 40 Hz firing example is a result of these teaching parameters, not a universal threshold for pyramidal neurons.

Reading the extracellular field

Blue and coral show negative and positive extracellular potential relative to an ideal distant reference. The optional arrows show electric-field direction, −∇V. The heatmap is calculated from the synaptic transmembrane currents plus equal return currents lumped near the soma. Pale shading indicates voltage near zero; stronger shading indicates larger voltage magnitude. The color scale adjusts to population size and stays fixed during playback and zoom, so large populations retain visible gradients. Compare absolute amplitudes using the labeled recording plots. It is not membrane voltage, transmitter concentration, or a pile of net charge.

The underlying current and voltage calculations are linked, but the spatial field is a qualitative homogeneous-conductor approximation in arbitrary units. It does not solve the detailed membrane current distribution, presynaptic Ca²⁺ currents, fields of spikes, or head tissues. The recording channels use the same synaptic source currents with an explicitly scaled homogeneous conductor; they are model predictions, not fitted human EEG amplitudes. Ca²⁺ entry is inward positive current and does create a local extracellular sink; its absence from this map is a model omission.

What is left out?

Stochastic vesicle release and failures; short-term facilitation/depression; long-term plasticity; NMDA and GABA-B receptors; active dendritic spikes and backpropagation; adaptation; glial uptake dynamics; and changes in ion gradients. Fixed gradients and a leak reversal represent the resting state. Pumps maintain gradients over time; they do not directly generate each rapid action-potential upstroke or downstroke. These are useful extensions, but are not needed for this first demonstration of temporal summation and inhibition.

Electrodes and signal display

Electrode 1 reads the highlighted cell’s somatic membrane voltage in mV: inside minus just outside. Electrode 2 is an extracellular point probe near the excitatory dendrite, measured relative to an ideal distant zero reference.

For the extracellular recording, a drawing distance of 100 world units represents 0.2 mm. The conductor has conductivity 0.3 S/m; source currents are converted from pA to A and potentials from V to µV using the point-source Green’s function. The probe lies 0.05 mm out of the source plane, with a 0.02 mm regularization core. No amplifier, electrode impedance, noise, or tissue boundaries are modeled.

The two channels share a time axis but retain their own labeled vertical units and scales. Intracellular positive voltage is upward. The “− up” option reverses only the extracellular channel, as commonly displayed in EEG. “Fit visible” uses past visible data, not future events. EEG view applies a causal 0.5 Hz high-pass and two-stage 40 Hz low-pass to the extracellular channel; it is a teaching filter, not a particular clinical amplifier. The heatmap stays raw when the recorded field is filtered, so removing the DC baseline can change a trace’s sign relative to the map.

How rhythm presets generate their signals

Delta, theta, alpha, and beta presets recruit 4,096, 2,048, 2,048, and 1,024 pyramidal neurons, respectively. Their excitatory and inhibitory inputs are rate-modulated around 2, 6, 10, or 20 Hz. A small number of representative conductance-based neurons (up to 24) samples different spike phases and slightly different modulation phases; each represents an equal fraction of the population. The extracellular trace is calculated by summing their synaptic currents and return currents through the electrode model. No sine wave is added to the recording channels.

The driving rate envelopes include harmonics, mild frequency variability, and amplitude changes. Delta has broad asymmetric cycles; theta has uneven rounded cycles; alpha waxes and wanes with mild asymmetry; beta occurs in brief faster packets. These are selected examples, not fixed shapes that define a frequency band. Alpha is not necessarily the arch-shaped sensorimotor mu rhythm. The presets are externally driven population examples, not self-organizing thalamocortical or recurrent-network generators, and they do not imply a universal E:I ratio. Rate controls become peak input rates in rhythm mode. Selecting a preset shows a few seconds of already-simulated activity and uses real-time playback so the rhythm is immediately visible.
